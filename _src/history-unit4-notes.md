## Deck subtitle
Jacksonian Democracy, Westernization, & Sectional Tensions

## 1. Universal White Manhood (Male) Suffrage
- "Age of the Common Man"
- Increased the electorate: all white men could vote regardless of property ownership; happened between the 1824 and 1828 elections
- Andrew Jackson benefited from a more "diverse" electorate
- 3 times as many people voted in 1828

## 2. Andrew Jackson / Jacksonian Democracy
- Democratic Party. Elected 1828 after losing to John Quincy Adams in the controversial 1824 election
- Campaign: painted JQA as elitist; painted himself as humble (though he was a wealthy plantation owner)
- Spoils System: rewarded campaign supporters with public office
- Frequent veto use, most of any president to that point: GW 2, J. Adams 0, Jefferson 0, Madison 7, Monroe 1, JQA 0, Jackson 12
- Overview: personified the "democratic spirit" by challenging the economic elite (Bank of the US fight). Politics changed: more equality for adult white males (property requirements dropped) → more voter participation; rise of interest-group politics and sectional issues; new campaign style
- One-pager (Jackson): Strategy, Spoils System, Economic: opposed the Bank of the US; Native American relations: wanted tribes moved west, supported the Trail of Tears

## 3. Indian Removal Act (1830)
- Jackson wanted tribes moved farther west (didn't think "they could assimilate with white settlers")
- Tribes had to sign treaties with the federal government and move west
- Cherokee sued Georgia to keep their land; Chief Justice John Marshall ruled the Supreme Court had no original jurisdiction: "Cherokees were a dependent nation"
- Confined to reservations; Trail of Tears killed up to one-third of those moved

## 4. Nullification Crisis
- 1828 tariff on imports to protect American (Northern) business; South had to buy pricier New England goods → "Tariff of Abominations"
- VP John C. Calhoun (SC): a state can nullify/reject a federal law it considers unconstitutional; if not allowed, the state may withdraw from the Union
- Jackson wanted to preserve the Union
- 1832: South Carolina threatened to secede rather than pay; Jackson ready to fight SC (Force Bill per one-pager)
- Senator Henry Clay proposed a bill lowering tariffs (Compromise Tariff, 1833) → crisis between federal authority and states' rights temporarily under control
- One-pager: a sectional crisis between SC and the federal government over protective tariffs and states' rights; causes: Tariff of 1828; outcome: compromise tariff

## 5. Bank War
- Bank of the US favored by the rich, unpopular with the poor
- Jackson vetoed the Bank's charter and moved federal money to state "pet banks"
- Pet banks printed money without gold/silver backing → money worthless → Panic of 1837: banks closed, savings lost, businesses bankrupt, 1/3 of population out of work
- Whig Party officially emerges from people unhappy with Jackson: supported temperance and using government for economic development; favored protective tariff, federal money for roads and canals, federal control of banking (Henry Clay)

## 6. Westernization
- Settlers moved west for economic opportunity (land to own and farm): Midwest, Southwest, Texas
- Railroads and canals grew an industrial economy and supported westward movement (Erie Canal; roads, canals, railroads)

## 7. Manifest Destiny
- Belief that America had the right to stretch from the Atlantic to the Pacific; gave political support for territorial expansion
- James K. Polk (Democrat) elected on a Manifest Destiny platform
- Rapid expansion disturbed the economies and cultures of indigenous peoples everywhere; settlers often pushed illegally onto American Indian land
- One-pager: belief that America could stretch Atlantic to Pacific; disturbed indigenous peoples; Polk elected on it; argued that expansion brought liberty/democracy; Mexican-American War (1846–48) → Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo; caused sectional tensions (would new lands be slave or free) → helped lead to Civil War

## 8. Texas, Oregon, Mexican-American War, Polk
- Americans migrated into Texas (land for cotton plantations); Mexico's constitution dissolved → armed revolt against Mexican rule
- Battle of the Alamo (San Antonio); Texans eventually won → independent republic for ~10 years, then joined the US (1845 annexation; the wait was tied to the slave/free balance from the Missouri Compromise)
- Mexican-American War: US victory → Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848) → Mexican Cession: California and the US Southwest (AZ, NV, NM, UT). War started over the Texas border (Rio Grande vs Nueces River, per the map assignment)
- Polk achieved his major goals in one term: Texas, the Southwest, California, and Oregon (settled the disputed Oregon Country with Great Britain, risking war; Treaty of Oregon 1846 set the US–Canada border)
- Made the US poised to become a world power, but sectional divisions got much worse
- One-pager (Territorial Expansion): Texas Annexation (1845), Oregon Country (1846), Mexican Cession (1848)
- California Gold Rush, the 49ers (map list); Gadsden Purchase 1853 completes the "puzzle pieces" of 1803–1853

## 9. Sectionalism & Sectional Tensions
- Sectionalism = loyalty to one's region rather than the country as a whole (political approach); regionalism = economic/cultural approach
- Union: Nullification Crisis; SC leaders sought to check federal power they feared might interfere with slavery's expansion
- Economic: North favored high protective tariffs (protect manufactured goods); plantation South opposed high tariffs (made imports more expensive); open West: emerging economy, supported internal improvements (roads, railroads, canals) and the sale of public land
- Westward expansion: new states kept the balance of free vs slave states in Congress; slavery spread west quickly; compromises tried to defuse crises

## 10. The three compromises (teacher has a "Comparing Compromises Card")
- **Missouri Compromise (1820):** east–west line through the Louisiana Purchase; slavery banned above, allowed below (Missouri slave, Maine free)
- **Compromise of 1850:** California entered as a free state; the Southwest territories from Mexico would decide for themselves by **popular sovereignty** (residents vote for or against slavery before statehood; major supporter Senator Stephen Douglas of Illinois); Fugitive Slave Law; slave trade outlawed in DC. One-pager also mentions Utah and New Mexico would decide
- **Kansas-Nebraska Act (1854):** repealed the Missouri Compromise line; gave Kansas and Nebraska popular sovereignty → bloody fighting between pro- and anti-slavery forces = "Bleeding Kansas"; led to the birth of the Republican Party that year

## 11. Slavery & resistance
- Slave revolts: Gabriel Prosser (1800, organized but didn't happen); Nat Turner (1831, revolted and fought). Fed white Southern fears of rebellion → severe restrictions on free Black people, harsh fugitive laws, Southerners favoring abolition intimidated into silence
- Fugitive Slave Law (1850): escaped slaves in free states had to be forcibly returned to owners in the South; slave trade outlawed in DC
- Caning of Charles Sumner (MA): fiery abolitionist accused pro-slavery senators of forcing Kansas into slavery, singled out Sen. Butler (SC); Butler's cousin Rep. Preston Brooks beat Sumner savagely → deeper North–South divide
- Dred Scott case (1857): Missouri slave sued for freedom after living in free states; Supreme Court under Chief Justice Roger Taney ruled Scott had no claim to freedom and the federal government could not prohibit slavery in the territories
- 1860 Census slide (image)

## 12. Abolitionists (one-pager: "2% of northern population, people who wanted to end slavery")
- Only ~2% of Northerners but got huge attention and anger in North and South
- Frederick Douglass: escaped slavery, wrote books and gave speeches against slavery and for equal rights
- William Lloyd Garrison: The Liberator; slavery violates Christian principles; Southerners alarmed at growing Northern response
- Harriet Beecher Stowe: Uncle Tom's Cabin, showed the horrors of slavery; fueled anti-slavery feeling in the North, infuriated Southerners
- Harriet Tubman: escaped; used the Underground Railroad to help many others escape; pitted Southern owners against Northerners who opposed returning escaped slaves
- John Brown: militant abolitionist; fought in Bleeding Kansas; raided the federal armory at Harpers Ferry hoping to start a massive slave rebellion; captured and executed

## 13. Second Great Awakening & reform
- Religious movement → period of social reform; optimism about moral reform through volunteer organizations
- Sparked: abolitionist, temperance, and women's suffrage movements
- Temperance (began 1820s): from moderation to total prohibition of alcohol; aiming for a perfect society; US, UK, British colonies; Lyman Beecher: "inebriation as a national sin"
- Women's suffrage: equal rights for women; Seneca Falls Declaration (Convention) 1848; Elizabeth Cady Stanton & Susan B. Anthony (active before and after the Civil War)

## 14. Political parties 1824–1856 (teacher worksheet)
- Democratic-Republicans split → Adams-Clay Republicans → National Republicans; Jacksonians → Democrats
- Other parties: Anti-Masonic, Nullifier, Whig, "Know Nothings" (American Party), Free Soil, Liberty
- Republican Party (1854): formed from Northern Whigs and antislavery Democrats (one-pager: people who opposed expanding slavery into territories, not abolition); opposed the Kansas-Nebraska Act and slavery's extension; supported free labor, economic opportunity, railroads, industrial growth, higher tariffs; leaders Abraham Lincoln, William H. Seward; supporters: farmers, workers, business owners, abolitionists

## 15. Lincoln-Douglas Debates & "A House Divided"
- 1858 in Illinois: Stephen Douglas (Northern Democrat, popular sovereignty) vs Abraham Lincoln (Republican, opposed spreading slavery to new states)
- "A house divided against itself cannot stand. I believe this government cannot endure, permanently, half slave and half free… It will become all one thing or all the other." Students are asked to analyze it.

## 16. Election of 1860 & secession
- 81.2% turnout; 152 electoral votes needed
- Lincoln (Republican) 180; Stephen Douglas (Northern Democrat) 12; John Breckinridge (Southern Democrat) 72; John Bell (Constitutional Union) 39
- One-pager: Lincoln won with no Southern electoral votes / far less of the popular vote than all opponents combined; Southern states began to secede, fearing Lincoln would end slavery
- South Carolina voted to secede, followed by 6 more Southern states; they feared Lincoln would abolish slavery; South believed it joined the Union freely and could leave
